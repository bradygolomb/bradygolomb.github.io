LiDAR Wound Scanning App

An iPhone app that measures and classifies wounds using the phone's depth sensor, scored against a clinical matrix built from fifty hours of watching physicians work.

Duration

Summer 2026

The problem

Wound assessment is one of the least standardized things a clinician does. Two providers looking at the same wound can record different measurements, different depth estimates, and different judgments about how it's healing — and because the record is usually a photograph plus a written note, there's no reliable way to compare a wound to itself two weeks later. For a healthcare group operating across many acquired practices, that variation compounds: the same patient can get materially different care depending on which office they walk into.

I spent the first weeks of the internship shadowing physicians rather than building anything, logging more than fifty hours across clinical settings. Watching the assessment happen repeatedly is what made the gap obvious. The measurement step was fast, informal, and almost entirely dependent on the individual clinician's experience.

The approach

Recent iPhones carry a LiDAR sensor that most people only ever use for portrait-mode background blur. It produces a real depth map, which means a phone that's already in every clinician's pocket can capture wound geometry — area, perimeter, and depth — without any additional hardware, calibration markers, or cost.

I built the app around that sensor. It captures the wound, extracts the measurements from the depth data, and runs image analysis to classify what it's looking at. The output isn't a raw number dump; it's scored against a wound care matrix I wrote, which encodes the assessment criteria I'd watched physicians apply informally into something the software can evaluate consistently every time.

Writing the matrix was the part that depended most on the shadowing. It had to reflect how clinicians actually reason about a wound rather than how a textbook describes one, or providers would ignore the output and go back to eyeballing it.

The outcome

The result is a wound assessment that produces the same answer regardless of who is holding the phone, and a record that can be compared against itself over the course of treatment. Because it runs on standard hardware, rolling it out across acquired practices costs nothing beyond the software itself.

The broader lesson was about sequencing. I could have started building on day one and shipped something faster. The fifty hours of shadowing are what made the tool usable, and they're the reason the matrix reflects clinical judgment instead of my assumptions about it.
