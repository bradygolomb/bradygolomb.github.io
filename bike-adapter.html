Prosthetic Bike Adapter

A pedal coupling for cyclists with prosthetic limbs, redesigned around the failure mode that kept breaking the old one.

The problem

Penn ADAPT builds assistive devices for athletes with disabilities. Working with the Philadelphia Adaptive Sports Center, we took on an adapter that lets cyclists with prosthetic limbs connect to a bike pedal — a part that was already in use, and already failing.

The failure was specific: the screws stripped. Not occasionally, but regularly enough that replacements and repairs were running over a thousand dollars a year. For an adaptive sports program, that's equipment budget that should be going toward getting more people on bikes. For a rider, it's a part that can't be trusted mid-ride.

The approach

I started with the failures themselves rather than with a new design. Understanding why the screws were stripping — the loading, the geometry, and how the interface behaved under repeated pedaling cycles — was what determined whether a redesign would fix the problem or just relocate it.

From there I modeled a new adapter in SolidWorks and moved through physical prototypes quickly: carbon-fiber 3D printed parts for fast iteration and machined parts for testing under real load. Each round went back to the same question — is this failing the way the old one failed, or a new way?

Alongside durability, the redesign had to be easier to attach. A device that a rider needs help putting on defeats a lot of its own purpose, and ease of attachment was a design requirement rather than a bonus.

The outcome

The redesigned adapter eliminated the stripping failures completely, ending more than a thousand dollars a year in replacements and repairs, and it attaches more easily than the part it replaced. One unit was completed during the school year and the work is continuing.

In 2025 I moved into a project lead role on a related problem, working with the Philadelphia Adaptive Sports Center on adaptive shoelaces — a lacing system that lets athletes with physical disabilities dress themselves without assistance.
