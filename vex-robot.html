The challenge

“Spin Up” asked for a robot that could do three unrelated things well: score discs accurately at range, manipulate field rollers, and expand at the end of the match. Each one pulls the design in a different direction, and the match is short enough that a subsystem which works most of the time is effectively a subsystem that doesn't work.

I led the team through the full cycle — design, fabrication, programming, and competition strategy — across two seasons that produced two World Championship qualifications.

The build

We modeled the whole robot in Onshape before cutting anything. With a fixed parts budget and a short season, catching an interference or a clearance problem in CAD is worth far more than the time it costs — a rebuild mid-season is a week you don't get back.

The core subsystems were a flywheel shooter for disc scoring and a roller manipulation system for field control. Expansion went through several candidate mechanisms before we settled on one, evaluated less on peak performance than on whether it fired reliably under match conditions.

The autonomous routines got the most iteration. Autonomous is where matches are won, because it's the only phase where performance is fully determined by preparation. We tested until the roller scoring was consistent, and ended the season hitting it in 94% of runs.

The result

The team placed 11th out of more than 11,000 teams worldwide in the Skills Competition at the VEX World Championships, with high goal accuracy and autonomous roller scoring at 94% reliability.

Leading the team taught me as much as building the robot did. Delegating work to people with different skill levels, keeping the schedule honest, and making the call to abandon a mechanism that wasn't going to be ready in time are all things I've used since — most directly in project lead roles at Penn ADAPT.
