EMR with Patient Audio Briefings

A records system that turns a physician's clinical notes into a plain-language podcast the patient can listen to on the drive home.

Role

Product Development Intern

Organization

One Health Partners
Naperville, IL

Built with

EMR architecture · AI summarization · Text-to-speech

Duration

Summer 2026

The problem

Patients forget most of what they're told in an appointment. They're anxious, the visit is short, and the information arrives in clinical vocabulary. What they leave with is usually a printed after-visit summary written in the same register as the chart it came from — which is to say, written for another clinician.

Meanwhile the actual account of what happened already exists, in detail, in the physician's SOAP notes and the clinical staff's patient notes. The information isn't missing. It's just stored in a form the patient can't use.

The approach

I built a new EMR system and added a layer on top of it that reads the physician's SOAP notes alongside the clinical staff's patient notes and generates an audio briefing from them. The patient gets a short podcast explaining, in ordinary language, what was found, what was done about it, and what they need to do during recovery.

Audio was a deliberate choice rather than a novelty. A printed summary competes for attention with everything else a patient is carrying out of an appointment, and reading it requires sitting down with it. A recording can be played in the car, replayed a week later when the instructions have blurred, or handed to the family member who's actually managing the recovery.

The design constraint that mattered most was tone. The output had to translate the clinical record without editorializing on it or inventing reassurance the notes didn't support — the goal was to make the existing record legible, not to generate new medical content.

The outcome

The system produces a patient-facing explanation of care and post-discharge treatment automatically, from notes clinicians were already writing. There's no additional documentation burden — the physician's workflow doesn't change, and the translation happens downstream.

What I took from the project is that a lot of healthcare's communication problem isn't a data problem. The information a patient needs usually already exists somewhere in their record. The work is moving it across the gap between how clinicians write and how patients listen.
